import csv
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score
rows = list(csv.DictReader(open("labels.csv")))   # chat_id, human, judge
kappa = cohen_kappa_score([r["human"] for r in rows], [r["judge"] for r in rows])
print(f"kappa = {kappa:.2f}")   # gate on the judge only near the human ceiling

Post Shopify Engineering Case study Rippling on LangChain
Cohen's kappa measures agreement beyond chance: 0 is chance and 1 is perfect. Shopify's first Sidekick judge agreed with expert labels at a kappa of 0.02, which is close to chance; after calibration it reached 0.61, against a human ceiling of 0.69. The labels came from sampled real conversations, each labelled by at least 3 experts, and the swap test is simple: put a human into the judge group, and if people cannot tell who the judge is, trust it. Step 2 is the check you can run yourself: label about 100 refund chats, run the judge on the same chats, and compute kappa with scikit-learn. Shopify also trains with GRPO and saw reward hacking (opt-outs, tags, made-up IDs), which is one more reason a judge that scores rewards must be calibrated first. Step 3: Rippling layers its evals from mocks on every commit up to about ten scenarios that block a deploy, and step 4 closes the loop: failing production traces go back to the base, an agent proposes a fix and a human merges. Analogy: you calibrate a scale with known weights before you weigh parcels with it. A likely question is "How many labels do I need?": start with about 100 human-labelled refund chats and stop tuning the judge when its kappa nears the human ceiling.
