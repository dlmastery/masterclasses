import csv
import sys
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score

path = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "labels.csv"
with open(path, newline="") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))  # chat_id, human, judge
kappa = cohen_kappa_score([r["human"] for r in rows],
                          [r["judge"] for r in rows])
print(f"{len(rows)} chats, kappa = {kappa:.2f}")

Kappa is agreement beyond chance: 0 is chance, 1 is perfect.

Ground truth: sampled real chats, each labelled by 3 or more experts.

Post Shopify Engineering, 2025-08-26
Cohen's kappa measures agreement beyond chance: 0 is chance and 1 is perfect. Shopify's first Sidekick judge agreed with expert labels at a kappa of 0.02, which is close to chance; after calibration it reached 0.61, against a human ceiling of 0.69, the agreement between the experts themselves. The labels came from sampled real conversations, each labelled by at least 3 experts, and their swap test is simple: put a human into the judge group, and if people cannot tell who the judge is, trust it. The script on the right is the check you can run yourself: label about 100 refund chats, run the judge on the same chats, and compute kappa with scikit-learn; the lab has a labels.csv with sample rows. Analogy: you calibrate a scale with known weights before you weigh parcels with it. A likely question: "When is the judge good enough?" When its kappa nears the human ceiling; stop tuning there, because the humans do not agree more than that.
