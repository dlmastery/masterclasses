For question one, push for the mechanism: when two rewards are added, GRPO first optimises the one that is easier to get, and a short wrong answer still earns brevity. Interview · DPO

DPO grows the margin mostly by pushing the rejected reply down

What does DPO train on, and what does beta set?
Pairs: one prompt, a chosen and a rejected reply. No reward model, no sampling. beta is the KL strength: higher stays closer to the start model.
Support-reply DPO: logps/chosen falls. Is it a bug?
Not necessarily. The margin grows mostly because the rejected reply falls. Watch rewards/margins and an eval on real tickets.
When is the fall a problem, and what do you change?
When logps/chosen keeps falling and eval quality drops. Raise the SFT weight or beta.

This slide is the DPO primer the chapter needs before the log question. DPO trains on preference pairs: one prompt, a chosen reply and a rejected reply. Here the chosen reply is on-brand, which means the support team's approved tone: empathetic, specific, and it takes an action. There is no reward model and no sampling; the loss raises the chosen reply relative to the rejected one, measured against a frozen copy of the start model, and beta (0.1 here) sets how far the policy may move from that copy. For question two, the strong answer says that DPO works mostly by suppressing the rejected reply, so rewards/margins grows from 0 to 1.14 while logps/chosen also falls a little, from -182.4 to -184.5 in this illustrative log. For question three, a slow fall is normal; a problem is a fall that continues while eval quality on real tickets drops. Then raise the SFT weight in loss_weights, or raise beta. Analogy: you can win a debate by making the other side look worse, not by making your own case better. Likely follow-up: "Why is rewards/accuracies near 1.0 a warning?" On a tiny in-domain set it means memorisation, so check the eval split.
Interview · Agentic coding RL

Harden the grader before the coding policy learns to beat it

The policy passes the tests but breaks the task requirements. How do you catch it?
Add hidden requirement tests the policy never sees. TestJack: about 34.4% of "correct" trials broke a requirement.
The policy can reach the test files. What do you change?
Mount tests and graders read-only in the sandbox. Before training, have people try to beat the grader (Cognition).
How often do you audit a learned reward?
Often and mildly: score a small gold-labelled sample at every eval step. Stop when the gold and proxy rewards split.
rent = reward gained by gaming the grader, not by solving the task. RL turned 41-66% of proxy gain into rent.

This slide moves the reward-hacking question to agentic coding, which the whiteboard builds next. Look for layers, not one trick. Question one: tests the policy can see become a target, so keep hidden requirement tests. TestJack (arXiv 2610.10619) found that about 34.4% of coding trials judged correct broke a task requirement, and resolution fell from 50.6% to 33.2% once requirement tests ran. Question two: the policy must not be able to edit what scores it, so mount tests and graders read-only in the sandbox; Cognition also has people try to beat the graders before training. Question three: "rent" is reward gained by gaming the grader rather than by solving the task; the Rent-Seeking paper (arXiv 2610.19832) found RL turned 41 to 66% of the proxy-reward gain into rent, and that frequent mild audits beat rare severe ones. So score a small gold-labelled sample at every eval step and stop when gold and proxy split. Analogy: a student who learns the marker's habits gets a higher mark without better answers.
