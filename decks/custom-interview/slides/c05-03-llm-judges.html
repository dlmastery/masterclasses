Treat the LLM judge as an instrument you calibrate

An LLM judge scored the 400 refund cases. How do you trust it?
Measure Cohen's κ against 2+ experts (κ = agreement beyond chance; 0 = chance, 1 = perfect). Expert-expert κ is the ceiling. Shopify went from κ 0.02 to 0.61 under a 0.69 ceiling. A text-to-SQL judge had κ 0.04; Qwen3.6-27B reached 0.72 at about 1/300 of the cost.

Is a pinned, temperature-0 judge deterministic?
No. Pinned judges flip about 5% of verdicts, and about 40% on close-call items. An Arena-Hard re-run flipped 21.2% of prompt outcomes; the aggregate moved about 1 point. Run the judge 3 times, keep the majority, and report the flip rate.

Ten judges agree. Is that strong evidence?
Less than it looks. Judge errors correlate (mean ρ = 0.21), so n_eff = 10 / (1 + 9 × 0.21) ≈ 3.5 independent judges. Five judges give about 2.7. In one study, 60% of wrong answers sat in the high-consistency region.

n_eff.py · numpy 2.5.4
import numpy as np
def n_eff(err):  # err[j, i] = 1 if judge j is wrong on item i
    C = np.corrcoef(err)                # judge x judge
    k = C.shape[0]; rho = (C.sum() - k) / (k * (k - 1))
    return k / (1 + (k - 1) * rho)      # 10, 0.21 -> 3.46
Ask each question, let the candidate answer, then reveal the model answer. Q1 tests whether the candidate treats the judge as a measuring instrument. Cohen's kappa is agreement beyond chance: 0 means chance level, 1 means perfect. Measure it against several experts, and use the expert-expert kappa as the ceiling, not 1.0. The two groups in the chart come from different datasets: only Shopify reported a human ceiling. Analogy: you check a new thermometer against a reference before you trust a fever reading. Q2 removes the myth that temperature 0 is deterministic: re-run the judge, keep the majority verdict, and publish the flip rate, especially when judge verdicts become DPO labels. In the grid, grey is a stable verdict and red is a verdict that changed between runs. Q3 tests independence thinking: correlated judges give fewer votes than they seem to, and consistency is not correctness. Likely follow-up: "How many human labels do I need?" Plan at least 25% human-judge overlap on a stratified sample; at 5% overlap the wrong best judge was picked 65% of the time (2609.31857).
