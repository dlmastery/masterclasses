def all_evidence_recall(rows, k=20):
    ok = [set(r["required"]) <= set(r["retrieved"][:k])
          for r in rows]
    return sum(ok) / len(rows)

rows = [{"required": ["c12", "c40"],
         "retrieved": ["c12", "c7", "c3"]}]
print(all_evidence_recall(rows))   # 0.0
Ask the question and wait. First fix the metric words, because candidates mix them up. Hit rate at k counts a question as a hit when any one required chunk is in the top k. Recall at k is the share of all required chunks that are in the top k. All-evidence recall counts a question only when every required chunk is in the top k. In the diagram the claim needs two facts, the deductible and the flood exclusion; the top 20 holds only the deductible. So hit@20 is 1, recall@20 is 0.5, and all-evidence recall is 0, and the generator cannot answer correctly however good it is. Click 2 shows the three numbers and the five-line function: label the required chunk ids per question, then test that the required set is a subset of the top k. Click 3: arXiv 2610.26951 found that similarity retrieval finds any relevant chunk far more reliably than all required chunks, so answer-only evals hide grounding failures. Score retrieval first, then faithfulness, then rerank to a small top-n. Analogy: an open-book exam is fair only if the book holds every page you need.
