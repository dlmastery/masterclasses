Ask each question, let the candidate answer, then reveal the model answer. Q1 tests whether the candidate names architecture: the plan comes only from the user's request, retrieved text is data, tools have least privilege, a human confirms a send, and egress is allowlisted. A better system prompt alone is a weak answer, because the same injection succeeds about three times as often through the retrieved channel (TRACER, 2610.19133). In code, the planner sees only the user message and picks the tools; the writer reads the documents and has no tools. A pytest suite plants a canary string and attack texts in the KB and asserts that the canary never appears in the output. Q2 red flags are a service-account identity and a filter after generation; the strong answer filters inside both the ANN and BM25 queries with the end user's groups and backs it with a leak test in CI. A post-filter also leaks through counts and timing: "3 results hidden" tells the user that restricted documents match. Analogy for Q2: a librarian who checks your badge before fetching books, not one who fetches everything and then takes books out of your hands. Q3 tests layered defenses for a live store: an ingestion filter, a read-path demand monitor (100% caught at 1% FPR in 2608.16044), provisional visibility for new items, and a leave-one-source-out check for high-stakes answers, because one attacker source raised wrong answers from 0.01 to 0.68 and the answer cited a trusted-looking source (CiteShade, 2609.15660). Likely follow-up: "Does a prompt-injection classifier solve it?" No: one scored 0.994 recall in-distribution and near zero on fluent fact-poisoning (2610.28364).
