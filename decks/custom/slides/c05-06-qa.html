Interview: dataset design and drift

“We have 50k SFT rows, ready to train.” What do you ask?
coverage provenance
Which taxonomy leaves do the rows cover, and how many examples does each leaf have? What is the provenance and license of each subset? What is the synthetic share, and which generator made it? Which contract validates each shard? Which slices and min n does the eval split have? Is it decontaminated against the eval sets?
Your KS test flags drift on every column, every day. Why, and what do you do?
statistics
At a large n, a p-value test flags tiny shifts as significant. Use distances with thresholds: Wasserstein, Jensen-Shannon or PSI (Evidently switches above n = 1000). Monitor the descriptors that matter, and tie each alert to a slice metric that drops.
How do you find failing slices that you did not think of?
discovery
Cluster failing production traffic and its embeddings. Use Slice Finder or Domino-style discovery; Domino finds 36% of 1,235 planted slice settings, so treat it as a help, not a proof. Promote each recurring cluster to a declared slice with a predicate, a min n, an owner and a gate.
What does EU AI Act Article 10 ask of training data?
governance
Governance of the design choices, the data origin, the preparation steps such as labeling and cleaning, the assumptions, and a bias examination. The data must be “relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete”. A Data Card maps onto these items.
Ask each question, wait, and let the students answer before you show the model answer. Q1 is the most common design question: a strong candidate does not ask about the row count at all, but about coverage per leaf, provenance, the contract and the eval split. Q2 tests statistics hygiene: the answer is that significance is not importance, and alerts must connect to a metric that a user feels. Q3 tests whether the candidate knows that declared slices miss failures, and that discovery tools miss most planted slices too. Q4 is now common in EU and regulated-industry interviews. Analogy: a row count is the weight of a book; coverage is its table of contents. Likely follow-up: "How do you prove the data is sufficiently representative?" Show leaf coverage and slice metrics against a taxonomy agreed with the product and risk owners; there is no standard metric yet, which is an open challenge.
