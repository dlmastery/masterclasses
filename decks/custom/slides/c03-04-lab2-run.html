from transformers import AutoConfig
c = AutoConfig.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507")
r, h, ff = 16, c.hidden_size, c.intermediate_size   # r from lab3_qlora_claims.py
q, kv = c.num_attention_heads * c.head_dim, c.num_key_value_heads * c.head_dim
per_layer = r * ((h + q) + 2 * (h + kv) + (q + h)   # q, k, v, o
                 + 2 * (h + ff) + (ff + h))         # gate, up, down
print(per_layer, per_layer * c.num_hidden_layers)   # 36 layers

Loss is not the task metric. eval_loss averages over all completion tokens. A weak stop token barely moves it, so prose can still follow the JSON. Step 4 scores the JSON.
The first line of train.log is the one to check: 33,030,144 trainable parameters, about 0.8% of the 4.06 billion in Qwen3-4B. check_params.py recomputes that count from config.json, and the table shows the arithmetic: r times in plus out for q, k, v, o, gate, up and down gives 917,504 per layer, and 36 layers give 33,030,144. If your number is much smaller, target_modules did not match, and you know before you read any loss. The second panel is the head and tail of train.log, not output of check_params.py. The chart shows the first of three epochs: the loss falls from 0.902 to an eval_loss of 0.142; the run continues to epoch 3 and saves that adapter to final. But loss is not the business metric. eval_loss averages over every completion token, so one weak end-of-turn token hardly changes it, and the model can still write prose after the closing brace. Step 4 measures JSON validity on held-out notes. Think of the adapter as the steering wheel on a heavy truck: small, but it decides where the whole model goes. Likely question: "Why is QLoRA slower per step than LoRA?" Every forward pass dequantizes the NF4 weights. AQLoRA (arXiv 2608.23816, Aug 2026) keeps the layers with the highest NF4 error in fp16 and trains 11.3 ± 2.3% faster, at a cost of 0.1 to 1.5 accuracy points; its layer choice matches Unsloth's dynamic-4bit checkpoints.
