The grey square is one frozen weight matrix W with d equal to 4096. Step 1: LoRA adds two thin matrices, B and A, and trains only them; watch the trainable share as the rank moves from 4 to 64 and back to 16. At r = 16 it is 2·d·r over d squared, about 0.78% of the layer. Step 2: rsLoRA changes the scale from alpha over r to alpha over the square root of r, so high ranks keep a useful update size. Step 3: the variants. QLoRA keeps the base in 4-bit NF4, DoRA splits magnitude and direction, PiSSA starts from the top singular vectors. Step 4: the practical evidence from LoRA Without Regret: all layers, about 10 times the learning rate. Analogy: the base model is a printed book, and LoRA is a thin transparent overlay you can swap. Likely question: "Why does LoRA need a higher learning rate?" Because B starts at zero and the update is scaled down, so the same step size moves the output less.
