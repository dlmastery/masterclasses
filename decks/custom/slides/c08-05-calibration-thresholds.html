Calibrate the scores, then pick the threshold with the cost of each error

“Modern neural networks, unlike those from a decade ago, are poorly calibrated.”

Paper Guo et al., On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks (ICML 2017)

Expected calibration error (ECE)

ECE = Σb (|Bb| / n) · |acc(Bb) − conf(Bb)|

Use 10 or 15 equal-width bins. ECE depends on the binning, so also report the Brier score or log loss, and show a reliability diagram.

Fix it on a held-out split

Temperature scaling, a one-parameter form of Platt scaling, “is surprisingly effective”. Or use CalibratedClassifierCV(method="sigmoid") or "isotonic".

LLM uses: guardrail and classifier heads; verbalized or token-probability confidence for abstention; judge confidence to send hard items to humans.

A score is not a decision

With calibrated p, flag when

p > t* = CFP / (CFP + CFN)

A miss costs 20× a false alarm → t* = 1/21 ≈ 0.048.

EC(t) = π·FNR(t)·CFN + (1 − π)·FPR(t)·CFP

π is the prevalence in production. On the ROC curve, the optimum touches an iso-cost line of slope CFP(1 − π) / (CFN·π).

Golden sets are enriched with positives. Re-weight to production prevalence before you pick t. Check t per slice too: one global t can be unfair across slices.
pick_threshold.py · from 07-eval-design §7

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve
fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y_true, scores)
pi, C_fp, C_fn = 0.02, 1.0, 20.0  # prod prevalence, costs
ec = pi*(1-tpr)*C_fn + (1-pi)*fpr*C_fp
best = np.argmin(ec)
print(f"threshold={thr[best]:.3f} TPR={tpr[best]:.2f} "
      f"FPR={fpr[best]:.3f} EC={ec[best]:.4f}")

Two ideas: a probability must mean what it says, and a threshold must come from costs. Step 1 shows a reliability diagram for an overconfident model; in the top bin it claims about 95 percent confidence but is right only about 80 percent of the time, and the ECE sums those gaps. Step 2 applies temperature scaling on a held-out split, and the bars move onto the diagonal. Step 3 draws a ROC curve; every point on it is a possible threshold. Step 4 sweeps the cost ratio: when a miss costs 20 times a false alarm, the threshold drops to about 0.048 and the operating point climbs the curve. These curves are examples, not real model data. Analogy: a smoke alarm is set very sensitive because a missed fire costs far more than burnt toast. Likely question: "Why not just maximize F1?" F1 ignores the real costs and the production prevalence, so it picks the wrong point when errors cost different amounts.
