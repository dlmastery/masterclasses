What Should We Remove? Evaluating the Reliability and Costs of RLVR Data Decontamination

Over 1,673,514 items, 52 of 59 sampled removals at cosine 0.80 were false (88%). No threshold from 0.80 to 0.98 reached 90% precision. A judge-adjudicated policy: 98.1% precision, 98.2% recall.
Two stages: MinHash or embedding candidates, then adjudication. Log every removal.
Three papers from the last month that change how we build the HR eval and training sets. Step 1: quality filters such as FineWeb and DCLM let AI-written documents through 2.3 and 9.8 times as often as human ones, so about 31% of filtered web tokens in August 2026 were AI text; the 400 dots are a schematic, the rates are the paper's. Step 2: when you ask for 10 synthetic records in one call, earlier records steer later ones, and correlations come out 48 to 127% more wrong, so ask for one record per call. Steps 3 and 4: decontamination by cosine similarity at 0.80 removed mostly innocent items, 52 of 59 in the sample, while a judge-adjudicated policy reached 98.1% precision and 98.2% recall; that is why our Lab 9 MinHash step only proposes candidates. Analogy: a metal detector at the airport flags many belts, so a person checks each alarm before anyone is removed. Likely question: is adjudication too expensive? It runs only on the candidates, which are a small share of the corpus, and every removal is logged for audit.
